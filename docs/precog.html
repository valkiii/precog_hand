Precog

Wave at your Mac. Slide between desktops with three fingers in the air, pinch to click, grab a window and throw it to the other screen. A webcam, a model that finds 21 points on each hand, and a matcher that learns the gestures you teach it.

Prologue

The interface from the film, on a laptop

In Minority Report, detectives sort evidence by sweeping their hands through the air. The film needed gloves and a wall of glass. Today a laptop camera and an open hand-tracking model are enough to try the same idea on an ordinary desk.

Precog watches your hands through the webcam (or an iPhone acting as one), turns each frame into 21 points per hand, and compares what it sees with gestures you recorded. When something matches, it acts on macOS directly: desktops, windows, apps, clicks.

ACT I

Seeing

From camera pixels to a hand you can measure.

Hand tracking

Twenty-one points per hand

Each camera frame goes through Google's MediaPipe hand landmarker. It returns 21 points for every hand it finds: the wrist, four joints on the thumb, and four on each finger, plus a guess at which hand is left and which is right.

Precog never looks at pixels after this step. Everything that follows (poses, swipes, pinches, the standby check) is geometry on these points.

Hover a point to see its name.

Making hands comparable

Near or far, the same hand

A hand close to the camera looks twice as big as one at arm's length. So every hand is redrawn relative to its own wrist and divided by its own size: the distance from the wrist (point 0) to the middle-finger knuckle (point 9).

After that, a fist is a fist anywhere in the frame. Movement is measured the same way: the palm centre (the average of the wrist and the four knuckles) travels in hand-widths, not pixels. A swipe of four hand-widths is the same swipe whether you sit close or lean back.

ACT II

Recognizing

Comparing what the camera sees with what you taught it.

Three kinds of gesture

Poses, movements, and both hands

A pose is a hand shape held still: a fist, a V sign, the "telephone". It fires after you hold it for 0.45 seconds with the palm nearly still, and won't fire again until you let go.

A movement is a shape plus a path: a swipe, a push, a fist that springs open. Precog keeps the last few seconds of each hand and keeps asking whether the recent stretch looks like one of your recordings.

A two-hand movement adds the gap between the hands and the path of their midpoint. "Expand" (hands together, then apart) is mostly a growing gap. While both hands are in view, one-hand swipes are ignored so pulling your hands apart doesn't also switch desktops.

Dynamic time warping

A slow swipe and a fast swipe are the same swipe

People never repeat a movement at the same speed. Precog resamples every movement to 16 steps, then lines the live one up against each recording with dynamic time warping: every live step is paired with the recorded step it resembles most, as long as the order is kept.

The cost of a pairing is the difference in hand shape plus 0.35 × the difference in path. Averaged over the matched pairs, that gives one distance per recording. Below the gesture's threshold, it fires.

Teaching it

Six samples set the threshold

You record a gesture about six times. Recording starts by itself once the camera sees your hand; for movements you hold still, do the move, and hold still again.

Precog then measures how far each sample is from its nearest neighbour. A take that disagrees with the rest (a misread hand, a much slower attempt) is set aside: anything beyond three times the median spread. The threshold comes from the spread of what's left, with a floor and a ceiling so no gesture becomes impossible or too eager.

./precog-run list flags gestures whose samples disagree, so you know which ones to record again.

The gestures

What your hands can do

Three of these are built in (three-finger swipe, pinch, ten fingers). The rest start from poses you record yourself, so you can pick any shape that feels natural.

Built in · gesture mode

Three-finger swipe

Index, middle and ring finger up, then move sideways. The desktop slides with your hand; let go past a third of the way to switch.

Built in · manipulate

Pinch to click

Thumb to index fingertip, then release. The ring around the cursor fills as your fingers close. Thumb to middle finger is a right-click.

Built in · manipulate

Grab, drag, snap

Pinch on a window and move your hand. Drag into the left or right edge for half the screen, the top edge to maximize.

Your pose · app switcher

Telephone → apps

The strip locks open. Relax your hand, move left or right to choose, pinch to open. Folding into a fist never selects by accident.

Your gesture · layout

Expand → layouts

Hands together, then apart. Point at a cell to preview where the window will go; pinch to apply it.

Built in · any mode

Ten fingers → standby

Both hands, fingers spread wider than a normal open hand, held still for 0.8 s. Do it again to wake Precog up.

Under the trackpad

Sliding desktops the way a trackpad does

The obvious approach, pressing Ctrl+→ in software, does nothing: macOS ignores synthetic key presses for switching desktops. A real three-finger swipe sends something else, a private "dock swipe" event with a progress value that grows as your fingers travel.

Precog sends that same event sequence. It starts with Began, follows with a stream of Changed events while your hand moves, and ends with Ended to commit or Cancelled to spring back. On macOS 27 each event also has to carry a packed hardware record in a hidden field (4205); that part is ported from the open-source tool iss.

Clicking in mid-air

A pinch that behaves like a mouse button

The cursor follows your index and middle knuckles, which barely move when you pinch. As thumb and index close in, the cursor slows down; once they touch, it freezes, so the click lands where you aimed.

Releasing the pinch is the click, however long it lasted. It only becomes a drag once your hand moves more than 45 pixels while pinched. Two pinches in a row make a double-click. Pinch distance is measured in 3D, so fingers that merely overlap from the camera's angle don't count.

Under the hood

Two processes and three macOS doors

The main process reads the camera, runs the hand model and makes every decision. A second process does nothing but draw: a transparent, click-through window above everything, repainted 60 times a second, which holds the reticle, previews, banners and the hand skeleton in the corner. It also owns the ◉ menu-bar item.

To act on macOS, Precog uses three doors: the Accessibility API to move and resize windows, synthetic mouse events for clicks, and dock-swipe gesture events for desktops.

  • tracker.pyCamera, skips a dark camera automatically, MediaPipe landmarks
  • features.pyWrist-relative shapes, hand size, palm path, pinch in 3D
  • recognizer.pyPose holds, DTW for movements, outlier-proof thresholds
  • spaces.pyDock-swipe events with the macOS 27 payload
  • pointer.pyCursor, click on release, drag, edge snap, throw
  • overlay.pyEverything you see on screen, plus the menu bar
  • recorder.pyHands-free recording with on-screen guidance
  • switcher.pyApp strip and layout grid, anchored where your hand settles
  • doctor.pyChecks camera, hand detection, permissions, overlay
EPILOGUE

Your turn

Set it up in about ten minutes, most of it recording gestures.

  1. Check what you need

    A Mac with Apple silicon, Python 3.12 and a camera: the built-in one, or an iPhone through Continuity Camera. Precog picks the first camera that gives a real image.

  2. Install

    From the project folder, create an environment, install the dependencies and download the hand model (about 8 MB).

    $ uv venv -p 3.12 .venv
$ uv pip install -p .venv -r requirements.txt
$ curl -L -o hand_landmarker.task \
    https://storage.googleapis.com/mediapipe-models/hand_landmarker/hand_landmarker/float16/latest/hand_landmarker.task

  3. Grant two permissions

    In System Settings → Privacy & Security, allow Camera and Accessibility for the terminal app you run Precog from. Then check that everything is wired up:

    $ ./precog-run doctor

  4. Record the starter pack

    A guided session suggests swipes, expand, a pointer pose, the telephone and more. A camera window opens and records each gesture six times; you answer n to skip any you don't want.

    $ ./precog-run setup

  5. Rehearse, then go live

    The test run shows the camera view with a match meter per gesture and doesn't act on anything. When you're happy, run it for real: no camera window, just the overlay and the ◉ menu-bar item.

    $ ./precog-run test     # recognise, don't act
$ ./precog-run          # live · ◉ menu bar to quit
Make it yours

Teach a new gesture in a minute

The wizard asks for a name, the kind (pose, movement or two-hand), and what it should do, from a list of 31 actions: Mission Control, any keyboard shortcut, open an app, run a shell command or AppleScript, media keys, window layouts and more.

If a gesture fires too easily or not enough, adjust its sensitivity or add more samples instead of starting over.

$ ./precog-run new                     # guided
$ ./precog-run new circle --kind motion --action mission_control
$ ./precog-run record "swipe left" --append
$ ./precog-run sensitivity "swipe left" 1.3
$ ./precog-run bind "app switch"       # change its action
$ ./precog-run list                    # flags inconsistent samples
$ ./precog-run calibrate               # fit ten-finger standby to you
